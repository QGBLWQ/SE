ABSTRACT Deep learning-based sound source localization systems typically rely on specific microphone
array configurations, making them highly vulnerable to hardware failures. Unexpected sensor faults cause
severe structural signal degradation, leading to substantial deterioration in localization accuracy. To address
this challenge, we propose a robust Fault-Resilient Sound Source Localization framework that autonomously
diagnoses sensor faults and adapts the model at test time without requiring ground-truth labels. The
proposed system integrates two core modules: 1) a Lightweight Fault Diagnosis module, which employs
a computationally efficient Frequency-Time Projection Network with a Fault Queue mechanism, and 2) a
Diagnosis-Guided Confidence-Aware Test-Time Adaptation strategy, which leverages the highly reliable
diagnosis results to apply interventions and utilizes a confidence thresholding mechanism to generate refined
hard pseudo-labels for stable self-training. As a result, the proposed system reduces the mean absolute error,
averaged across diverse models and fault conditions, from 21.99 ◦ to 9.91 ◦, achieving a significant error
reduction of 54.92%, thereby ensuring reliable robotic perception even in the presence of sudden hardware
malfunctions. The proposed framework is further validated in a real-environment experiment using a physical
microphone array with an authentic hardware fault, confirming its generalizability beyond synthetic fault
conditions.

I. INTRODUCTION
Sound source localization (SSL) is a fundamental capability
for robotic perception and human-computer interaction,
enabling embedded systems such as service robots and smart
speakers to detect acoustic events and interact with users
implicitly [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]. Encompassing diverse
tasks ranging from direction-of-arrival (DOA) estimation to
environment-aware localization and acoustic mapping, these
systems typically rely on multi-channel audio captured by
microphone arrays, where spatial information is encoded in
the time and phase differences between microphones.

.
Historically, it is well established that the accuracy of
SSL is closely tied to the number of microphones [7], [8];
increasing the number of sensors generally improves spatial
resolution but imposes a significant burden on computational
cost and memory usage. For instance, high-end commercial
arrays like the 64-channel Eigenmike can achieve precise
localization but require processing power often unavailable
on resource-constrained embedded platforms [9]. To address
this trade-off, recent advancements in deep learning have
enabled robust SSL performance using compact array
configurations [1], [2], [3]. Thanks to these data-driven
approaches, it is now possible to achieve high-precision 2D
DOA estimation (e.g., mean absolute error (MAE) of DOA
of 2.65◦ [3]) using as few as four microphones.

However, this reliance on minimal sensor configurations
introduces a critical vulnerability: the system becomes highly
sensitive to hardware failures. In real-world deployment
scenarios, microphones are prone to various physical and
electrical faults caused by hardware damage (e.g., broken
wires), connection instability, or aging of vibrating mem-
branes [10]. As detailed in Sec. III-B, these hardware issues
manifest as distinct fault patterns, including No Sound,
Attenuation, Impulsive Noise, Continuous Noise, and Signal
Replication. The impact of such failures is substantial
for deep learning-based models trained on healthy data.
As demonstrated in our empirical analysis in Sec. VI-B2,
a single microphone failure in a 4-channel array causes the
MAE of DOA estimations, averaged across diverse models
and fault conditions, to surge by approximately 238% (from
6.50◦ to 21.99◦), rendering the localization system unreliable.
Even in the event of sudden hardware failure, autonomous
systems must maintain an acceptable level of stability and
functionality until repair or replacement can be performed.
If a robot malfunctions due to an unexpected sensor fault,
it could lead to erroneous decision-making, potentially
resulting in safety accidents involving humans or damage
to the environment. Consequently, extensive research has
been conducted on fault detection and diagnosis (FDD) to
ensure the reliability of various sensors, such as LiDAR [11],
cameras [12], and IMUs [13], utilizing deep learning to
handle complex signal patterns. However, research specif-
ically addressing microphone array faults remains limited.
While methods for diagnosing microphone faults exist, they
often rely on traditional signal processing features, such as
voice activity detection (V AD) inter-correlation [10]. These
conventional approaches struggle to detect phase-related
faults, such as channel copying (signal replication), because
the replicated signals retain valid speech characteristics and
energy levels.
Even if the microphone fault is accurately detected and
diagnosed, a critical challenge remains in model recovery.
Fine-tuning deep learning-based SSL models on corrupted
multi-channel audio involves a significant domain shift,
which typically requires access to ground-truth labels from
the target domain. To overcome this limitation, extensive
research has been conducted on test-time adaptation (TTA),
which enables models to adapt to distribution shifts using
only unlabeled test data [14], [15], [16], [17], [18], [19],
[20], [21].
However, directly leveraging these existing TTA frame-
works for SSL presents a non-trivial challenge. Most TTA
methods are tailored for classification tasks, relying on
entropy minimization or confidence maximization over
discrete label spaces, which do not straightforwardly translate
to the continuous regression nature of DOA estimation.
Although recent studies have extended TTA to structured
regression problems in computer vision—such as depth
completion [22], optical flow [23], and pose estimation [24],
[25]—these approaches predominantly exploit visual geo-
metric constraints. Consequently, since these methods are
inherently designed for the visual domain, they are not
directly applicable to audio-based SSL tasks that require
handling unique spectral and spatial degradations, particu-
larly when hardware failures disrupt the inter-channel phase
relationships.
To this end, we propose a robust Fault-Resilient SSL
framework that can autonomously detect the specific
fault type and adapt the pre-trained model to the target
domain without external intervention. Previous research on
microphone fault diagnosis has primarily focused on specific
features, while existing TTA approaches are ill-suited for
SSL under hardware faults as they do not account for
the structural signal degradation. This paper presents a
tightly coupled fault-resilient SSL framework in which fault
semantics explicitly guide the adaptation process.
The main contributions of this paper are summarized as
follows:
• Lightweight and Reliable Fault Diagnosis
(Sec. IV-A): We propose a computationally efficient
Frequency-Time Projection Network integrated with a
robust Fault Queue mechanism to ensure precise fault
identification.
• Diagnosis-Guided Confidence-Aware TTA
(Sec. IV-B): We propose a confidence-aware TTA
framework based on hard pseudo-labeling, which
enables stable self-training for SSL under microphone
faults. Furthermore, we introduce a diagnosis-guided
adaptation strategy that leverages fault semantics to
enhance robustness against severe structural
degradations.
We rigorously validate both the fault diagnosis and adap-
tation modules with synthetically injected faults (Sec. VI).
First, we verify that the proposed Lightweight Fault Diag-
nosis model can be stably trained to yield precise fault
classification in Sec. VI-A. Subsequently, through extensive
experiments in Sec. VI-B, we demonstrate the superiority
of our TTA method over the baselines; with all proposed
components enabled, the system achieves a significant error
reduction of 54.92% (MAE improving from 21.99◦ to 9.91◦),
averaged across diverse models and fault conditions.
We further confirm the practical stability of our system
for real-time embedded deployment through sensitivity anal-
yses covering threshold initialization, dynamic thresholding,
silence threshold, batch size, and threshold sensitivity (from
Sec. VI-B3 to Sec. VI-B7), and analyze the system’s
robustness under misdiagnosis scenarios in Sec. VI-B8.
Beyond single-channel failure conditions, we extend our
evaluation to simultaneous dual microphone failure scenarios
in Sec. VI-C, identifying the boundary between recoverable
and irrecoverable failure states imposed by the geometric
constraint requiring at least three operational microphones
for reliable 2D DOA estimation. Finally, we validate the
generalizability of the framework beyond the SSLR dataset
through a real-environment experiment using a physical
microphone array with an authentic hardware fault in
Sec. VI-D.

II. RELATED WORKS
A. SOUND SOURCE LOCALIZATION
Research in SSL can be broadly categorized into DOA
estimation, environment-aware localization, and mobile
robot-based acoustic mapping.
DOA estimation has been extensively studied as a core
problem in SSL, and recent advances in deep learning
have significantly accelerated progress in this direction.
By learning the mapping between acoustic features and
source directions, deep learning-based approaches have
demonstrated improved robustness to noise and reverbera-
tion, while enabling accurate localization even with a small
number of microphones, such as compact and minimally
redundant microphone arrays. He et al. [1] employed
deep neural networks for multiple speaker detection and
localization, demonstrating robust performance in noisy
conditions. Wang et al. [2] introduced a mechanism to apply
attention to speech-dominant frequency bins in generalized
cross correlation-phase transform (GCC-PHA T) features,
enhancing localization accuracy. Furthermore, An et al. [3]
proposed a microphone pair training strategy to achieve
robust SSL across diverse array configurations, reducing the
dependency on specific array geometries during training.
Beyond DOA estimation, researchers have leveraged envi-
ronmental cues and auxiliary sensor data to handle complex
acoustic scenes. An et al. [4], [5] incorporated diffraction and
reflection models into the localization framework, utilizing
back-propagation signals and environmental geometry to
localize sources even in non-line-of-sight conditions. In a
data-driven context, Liu et al. [6] proposed a self-supervised
incremental learning framework to adaptively learn the
acoustic-to-location mapping in complex indoor environ-
ments, reducing the reliance on extensive annotated data.
For mobile robots, integrating spatio-temporal information
over multiple frames is crucial for consistent tracking.
Su et al. [26] proposed a real-time 3D sound source mapping
framework using linear arrays, accumulating DOA estimates
while the robot moves. More recently, Fu et al. [27]
introduced I-ASM, an iterative acoustic scene mapping
approach that enhances auditory perception in complex
indoor environments by continuously refining the acoustic
map.
Despite these advancements, a fundamental assumption
across these methods is the structural integrity of the micro-
phone array. Consequently, any deviation from the expected
configuration, such as hardware failures (e.g., dead channels,
gain mismatch), disrupts the spatial correlation structure,
leading to severe degradation in localization accuracy. This
vulnerability has received limited attention in existing SSL
literature, yet poses a significant hurdle for long-term robot
operation. We experimentally quantify this performance drop
caused by sensor faults in Sec. VI-B.
B. SENSOR FAUL T DETECTION AND DIAGNOSIS
Hardware devices operating in real-world environments
are prone to unexpected failures. Consequently, substantial
research has been conducted on fault detection and diagnosis
(FDD) to ensure system reliability and safety [28], [29].
With the recent advancements in artificial intelligence, deep
learning-based FDD has been actively investigated to handle
complex signal patterns.
For instance, Zhao et al. [30] proposed a method using
strong residual convolutional neural networks (CNNs) with
adaptive thresholds to reliably diagnose faults in rotating
machinery (e.g., motors) from vibration signals, even under
noisy conditions. Similarly, Mezair et al. [31] introduced
a hybrid model combining CNN, long short-term memory
(LSTM), and graph neural network (GNN) to detect anoma-
lies in industrial processes using heterogeneous time-series
sensor data. Ali et al. [32] utilized short-time Fourier
transform (STFT) and ensemble deep learning for motor
fault diagnosis. In the acoustic domain, Ha et al. [33]
employed a microphone array and a thermal imaging camera
to detect faults in transmission lines. While these studies
demonstrate the efficacy of deep learning in industrial
diagnosis, their primary objective is to identify anomalies
in the surrounding environment (e.g., motor malfunction,
transmission line defects). Consequently, they typically
operate under the assumption that the sensors themselves
are functioning correctly, leaving the issue of internal sensor
failures unaddressed.
Distinct from fault diagnosis targeting external environ-
mental phenomena, a separate line of research has focused on
ensuring the integrity of the sensing hardware itself. In visual
perception, research has been conducted to detect and handle
lens soiling in LiDAR [11] and camera sensors [12]. For
satellite attitude determination, Chen et al. [13] proposed a
recovery method fusing Kalman filters and neural networks
to handle IMU sensor faults. In multi-modal sensor fusion,
Zhang et al. [34] demonstrated robust performance mainte-
nance even when a specific sensor (e.g., camera) fails or is
missing. Cicero et al. [35] addressed anomaly detection in
label-scarce IoT sensor networks using a U-Net architecture
and sliding window-based online learning. Furthermore,
in the context of antenna arrays, which share similar
array signal processing principles with microphone arrays,
Zheng et al. [36] utilized a ResNet architecture to robustly
detect faults in noisy environments.
Despite the critical impact of array integrity on SSL
performance, the majority of existing FDD research using
microphone arrays remains focused on localizing the source
of mechanical faults rather than diagnosing the microphone
array itself [33], [37], [38], [39]. While methods for
diagnosing microphone faults exist, they often rely on
traditional signal processing features, such as V AD inter-
correlation, which are insufficient for detecting phase-related
faults like channel copying, where replicated signals preserve
valid spectral and energy characteristics [10]. Recently,
Zhou et al. [40] addressed DOA estimation under array
imperfections via supervised transfer learning. Although this
approach attempts to adapt to the target domain, it does
not address automatic fault detection and requires labeled

C. TEST-TIME ADAPTATION
Since ground-truth labels are unavailable for corrupted
target-domain data during deployment, adaptation must be
performed in an unsupervised manner. Test-time adaptation
(TTA) has emerged as a promising paradigm to address
this by adapting models to shifted target domains without
access to the target labels. Traditionally, the majority of TTA
methodologies have been developed primarily for classifica-
tion tasks. Early approaches focused on entropy minimization
to reduce uncertainty in discrete prediction distributions,
as demonstrated by TENT [14] and MEMO [15]. Others
addressed catastrophic forgetting by selecting reliable low-
entropy samples [16] or mitigating sharp loss minima [17].
In parallel, pseudo-labeling strategies have been actively
explored for classification, utilizing techniques like nearest
neighbor information [18], teacher-student architectures
(CoTTA) [19], and confidence-based filtering [21] to guide
self-training.
Recognizing the need for adaptation in continuous output
spaces, recent efforts have extended TTA to regression
problems, particularly within the computer vision domain.
Unlike classification, these tasks require estimating contin-
uous values where standard entropy minimization is not
directly applicable. Representative examples include depth
completion [22], optical flow estimation [23], and pose
estimation [24], [25]. These methods typically leverage
structural constraints or auxiliary guidance specific to visual
modality (e.g., geometric consistency) to drive the adaptation
process.
However, directly applying these existing TTA frame-
works to SSL remains non-trivial due to inherent task
and domain discrepancies. First, SSL is fundamentally a
regression problem that estimates continuous source loca-
tions (e.g., DOA). This precludes the direct applicability of
prevalent classification-based TTA methods, as their core
mechanisms—such as minimizing the entropy of discrete
class probabilities—do not straightforwardly translate to
continuous regression outputs. Second, adaptation strategies
tailored for vision-based regression do not readily generalize
to audio-based SSL, especially under microphone array
failures. The spatial cues in audio signals (e.g., phase
differences) are fundamentally different from visual features.
Consequently, the direct transfer of methods designed for the
visual modality to the SSL problem is constrained by these
inherent domain discrepancies.
It is also important to distinguish TTA from fault-
augmented retraining, an alternative strategy that addresses
domain shifts by augmenting the training data with
fault-injected samples and retraining the model offline.
While fault-augmented retraining can improve robustness to
anticipated fault conditions, it requires access to ground-truth

III. PROBLEM FORMULATION
A. SOUND SOURCE LOCALIZATION TASK
We consider a robot equipped with a microphone array
consisting of M microphones. Let s(n) denote a sound source
signal in the time domain. Under the far-field assumption,
the signal received by the m-th microphone, xm(n), can be
modeled as:
xm(n) = s(n − τm(θ )) + vm(n), (1)
where τm(θ) is the time delay of arrival relative to the
source direction θ and the m-th microphone position, and
vm(n) denotes additive background noise. While the general
DOA θ encompasses both azimuth and elevation in 3D
space, we simplify the problem to 2D planar localization by
assuming the source and the robot lie on the same horizontal
plane. Thus, in this work, θ represents the azimuth angle
solely.
Following recent data-driven approaches [1], [2], [3],
we formulate the DOA estimation task as a supervised

B. MICROPHONE FAUL T TYPES
In practical deployment scenarios, microphone arrays are
susceptible to various hardware failures caused by physical
damage, circuit degradation, or environmental factors [10].
To systematically analyze the impact of these failures,
we model the faulty signal ˜xm(n) received by the m-th
microphone as a corrupted version of the clean signal xm(n):
˜xm(n) = αm(n) · xm(n) + βm(n), (4)
where αm(n) represents a (possibly time-varying) gain scaling
factor (where αm(n) = 1 denotes a healthy state) and βm(n)
represents additive fault noise.
Based on a prior study [10] and common hardware
failure modes, we categorize five representative fault types
commonly observed in practice, alongside the normal state
(No Fault), as visualized in Fig. 2.
• No Sound (Dead Channel): This fault occurs due to
disconnected cables, broken wires, or complete sensor
failure. Mathematically, this corresponds to a complete
loss of signal information, modeled as αm(n) ≈
0 and βm(n) ≈ 0. For a neural network, this results
in a ‘‘dead’’ input channel containing only zeros or
negligible thermal noise.
• Attenuation (Gain Mismatch): Microphone sensitivity
can degrade over time due to dust accumulation on
the acoustic port or aging of the vibrating membrane
(diaphragm). This results in a consistent reduction in
signal amplitude, modeled as 0 < α m(n) < 1 with
βm(n) = 0.
• Impulsive Noise (Pops & Clicks): Caused by loose
connections, electrical discharges, or mechanical
impacts, this fault introduces sudden, high-energy spikes
into the signal. Here, βm(n) appears as intermittent
impulsive noise with high amplitude, temporarily
overwhelming the source signal xm(n).
• Continuous Noise (White Noise): Degradation of inter-
nal amplifier circuits or electromagnetic interference
can inject persistent wide-band noise. In this case,
βm(n) is modeled as Gaussian white noise N (0, σ 2
fault )
with a variance significantly higher than the ambient
noise floor, severely degrading the signal-to-noise ratio
(SNR).
• Signal Replication (Cross-Channel Copy): This fault
occurs when the signal from one microphone (e.g.,
m-th mic) is erroneously duplicated to another channel
(e.g., j-th mic) due to data acquisition software errors
or short circuits in the signal path, i.e., ˜xj(n) =
xm(n), which cannot be captured by simple gain or
additive noise models. Crucially, this fault type is
difficult to detect using traditional methods. Previous
works relying on magnitude or V AD features [10] often
fail to identify this fault because the replicated signal
possesses valid speech characteristics and energy levels
(Sec. VI-A1). However, for SSL, this fault is particularly
detrimental, as it destroys the unique phase and time
difference of arrival (TDOA) information required for
DOA estimation.
The occurrence of any of these faults fundamentally
alters the input feature distribution Xt . Consequently, the
pre-trained mapping function fW (·), which assumes a healthy
array configuration, fails to extract correct spatial cues,
leading to a severe degradation in DOA estimation accuracy.
We will quantitatively analyze this performance drop in the
experimental results (Sec. VI-B2a).
While our primary analysis focuses on single-channel
failure scenarios, we additionally extend our evaluation
to simultaneous dual microphone failure conditions in
Sec. VI-C, identifying the practical boundaries of the
proposed framework. We specifically target a 4-channel
microphone array configuration, which is widely adopted
in robotic auditory perception [1], [2], [26]. This choice is
motivated by recent deep learning advancements demonstrat-
ing that, even with this compact setup, models can achieve
exceptional localization precision, reaching a MAE as low
as 2.65◦ [3]. However, maintaining at least three operational
microphones is the fundamental geometric prerequisite to
satisfy the constraint (M ≥ 3) defined in Sec. III-A, ensuring
unique 2D localization without front-back ambiguity. Sce-
narios involving two or more failed microphones generally
result in insufficient spatial information for reliable full 360 ◦
DOA estimation. Consequently, we evaluate the proposed
method under the single-fault assumption, which represents
the minimal viable configuration required to sustain the
robust performance of the target platform.
IV. FAULT-RESILIENT SOUND SOURCE LOCALIZATION
FRAMEWORK
In this section, we propose a Fault-Resilient SSL framework
that minimizes localization accuracy degradation under
abrupt hardware failures. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the
system operates as a sequential pipeline comprising two core
modules: 1) a Microphone Fault Diagnosis module driven
by a lightweight time-frequency network, and 2) a TTA
module based on confidence-aware self-training. Crucially,
the proposed framework is not a simple combination of fault
diagnosis and TTA, but a unified system in which diagnosed
fault semantics directly determine the form of intervention
and adaptation.

on resource-constrained edge platforms, the fault diagnosis
module must be computationally efficient while maintaining
high accuracy. Complex architectures such as Transformers
or deep CNNs often entail high computational costs, which
are unnecessary for the relatively distinct patterns of sensor
faults. Therefore, we propose a Lightweight Frequency-
Time Projection network (Fig. 4) that minimizes learnable

the TTA process only when the queue is fully populated
with an identical fault type (i.e., the same fault persists
for K consecutive frames). This temporal consistency check
ensures that the system reacts only to sustained hardware
malfunctions, effectively filtering out transient outliers.
B. DIAGNOSIS-GUIDED CONFIDENCE-AWARE TTA
Once a sustained microphone fault is confirmed by the
Microphone Fault Diagnosis module (Sec. IV -A), the system
triggers the TTA process. The overall workflow of the
proposed TTA framework is illustrated in Fig. 5. Leveraging
the probabilistic output representation defined in Sec. III-A,
our framework adapts the pre-trained model using its own
predictions. As discussed in Sec. III-A, this architectural
choice—predicting a spatial likelihood distribution rather
than a single coordinate—is the fundamental prerequisite
that allows us to quantify prediction uncertainty and enable
unsupervised adaptation.

the optimal decision boundary varies significantly across
different fault types (e.g., lower for signal replication,
higher for white noise). Second, the model’s confidence

flexibility to adapt to this evolution, potentially causing false
negatives in the early stages or admitting noise as the model
sharpens. To address these challenges and synchronize the

choice is grounded in the intrinsic behavior of the pre-trained

or shifted by hardware faults, the acoustic characteristic
of silence (i.e., near-zero input energy) remains largely

anchor to prevent the model from drifting into instability
by suppressing background noise. Therefore, we fix τsil =

to guarantee non-source regions while preserving potential
weak sources (detailed analysis in Sec. VI-B5).
4) DIAGNOSIS-GUIDED INTERVENTION STRATEGY
While the proposed TTA framework effectively recovers
performance across most fault scenarios, certain severe
faults—most notably Signal Replication—present a persis-
tent challenge. Even with the robust dynamic thresholding
described above, the recovery in these specific cases can
remain suboptimal compared to other fault types. This
is primarily because Signal Replication creates deceptive
spatial cues that mimic valid speech characteristics, making
it intrinsically difficult for the model to distinguish between
the true source and the hardware-induced ghost source purely
through self-supervision.
To overcome this limitation and further boost adaptation
performance, we leverage the precise fault classification
provided by our system to implement a Diagnosis-Guided
Intervention strategy (Fig. 5-A). Unlike general TTA
frameworks that adapt blindly to domain shifts, our approach
exploits the explicit knowledge of the fault type and the
affected channel index to apply targeted pre-processing
before the data enters the adaptation loop.
Specifically, for the Signal Replication fault, we intervene
by explicitly forcing the input of the diagnosed faulty channel
to zero (i.e., channel masking). This structural intervention
breaks the erroneous phase relationships caused by the
replicated signal, effectively converting a complex, deceptive
fault into a simpler dead channel (No Sound) scenario that
the SSL model can handle more robustly. The efficacy of
this diagnosis-guided intervention is quantitatively validated
in Sec. VI-B2e, where we demonstrate that removing the
deceptive artifacts leads to a substantial performance boost
in scenarios where the proposed TTA framework struggles.
More broadly, this result highlights that incorporating explicit
fault semantics can fundamentally reshape the adaptation
landscape, enabling recovery beyond the limits of data-driven
adaptation alone.
V. EXPERIMENTS
A. FAUL T DATA SYNTHESIS ON REAL-WORLD AUDIO
To validate the robustness of the proposed Fault-Resilient
Sound Source Localization Framework—which integrates
microphone fault diagnosis with TTA—in realistic acoustic
environments, we utilize the SSLR dataset [1] as our base
corpus. This dataset consists of real-world multichannel
audio recordings captured using a robot equipped with a
4-channel circular microphone array. Specifically, the record-
ings feature human speech generated by live talkers as well
as loudspeakers playing back recorded speech. Crucially, the
dataset encompasses varying acoustic complexities, ranging
from single-source scenarios to cases with two overlapped
sound sources, captured in diverse indoor environments with
varying levels of background noise and reverberation.
To simulate realistic failure scenarios, we synthetically
inject the five fault types defined in Sec. III-B into the clean
SSLR recordings.We specifically evaluate the system under
single-channel failure conditions, simulating the critical
state where the array operates with the minimum number
of sensors (M = 3). For a detailed discussion on the
geometric constraints and the rationale behind this single-
fault assumption, please refer to Sec. III-B.
While the primary evaluation focuses on single-channel
failure conditions, real-world deployments may encounter
simultaneous failures of multiple microphones. In particular,
when two microphones simultaneously lose all spatial
information (e.g., No Sound or Signal Replication), the
geometric constraints for reliable 2D DOA estimation can no
longer be satisfied, leading to severe localization degradation.
To investigate how the proposed Microphone Fault Diagnosis
model and TTA framework behave under such challenging
conditions, we extend our evaluation to a simultaneous dual
microphone failure scenario in Sec. VI-C.

Using the fault injection protocols described above, we gen-

each serving a specific purpose in our evaluation pipeline.
To ensure experimental integrity, we strictly adhere to the
original train/test splits of the SSLR dataset, preventing any
data leakage between the diagnosis model training (Dataset
A) and the TTA evaluation (Dataset B).
Dataset A (For Fault Diagnosis): This dataset is
constructed to train and evaluate the Microphone Fault
Diagnosis module (Sec. IV -A). To ensure the diagnosis
model generalizes across different sensors and failure modes,
we apply a randomized injection strategy. For each audio
sample in the SSLR corpus, we randomly select one target
microphone channel and apply one of six conditions: the
five fault types defined in the previous section or a No
Fault (Normal) condition. The inclusion of the No Fault
class is crucial, as it enables the diagnosis model to
discriminate between actual hardware failures and normal
acoustic variations.
Dataset B (For TTA): This dataset is designed to
simulate a realistic deployment scenario where the system
must sequentially diagnose a hardware failure and recover
performance via TTA (Sec. IV -B). Unlike the randomized
nature of Dataset A, Dataset B assumes a persistent failure on
a specific sensor during robot operation. Specifically, we fix
the fault location to the last microphone (m = 4) of the SSLR
test split and generate five independent evaluation sets, each
corresponding to one of the five fault types. This controlled
setup allows us to isolate the impact of each specific fault
type and quantitatively verify whether our framework—after
correctly diagnosing the faulty channel using the model
trained on Dataset A—can successfully recover localization
accuracy through adaptation.

B. IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS
To ensure the reproducibility of our experiments, we detail
the specific configurations for audio preprocessing, model
architectures, and training hyperparameters. All models
and experiments were implemented using the PyTorch
framework. Training and evaluation were conducted on a
workstation equipped with an Intel(R) Core(TM) Ultra 9
285K CPU and a single NVIDIA RTX 5090 GPU.
Audio Preprocessing: All audio recordings in the SSLR
dataset were resampled to 24 kHz. The input for both the
localization and diagnosis modules consists of 1-second
non-overlapping audio segments (24,000 samples). For the
spectral feature extraction used in the Fault Diagnosis
module, we compute the STFT to generate time-frequency
features with dimensions F = 257 (frequency bins) and
L = 50 (time frames).
Backbone SSL Models: To evaluate the generaliz-
ability of our framework, we employ three distinct SSL
architectures: MLP-GCC [1], GCC-PHAT-SM [2], and
MPT-HiFTA[3]. We adopt the exact network configurations
proposed in their respective original papers. In this work,
these models process a 1-second input frame to predict
a sequence of likelihood vectors corresponding to Q =
10 intra-frame steps. Consequently, the output dimension is
defined as Q × K (where K = 360). Prior to TTA, these

severe domain shifts. While hard labels force a sharp, decisive
update on the single most confident direction, soft labels may
inadvertently reinforce ambiguity or local noise surrounding
the peak, thereby hindering the rapid adaptation required for
fault recovery.

5) IMPACT OF SILENCE THRESHOLD IN TTA
The silence threshold τsil plays a pivotal role in distinguishing
active predictions from non-informative background noise.
To investigate the impact ofτsil on TTA performance, we con-
ducted a sensitivity analysis using the MPT-HiFTA model,
varying τsil within the range of [0.001, 0.1]. This analysis
encompasses all fault types, including Signal Replication,
to verify the universal applicability of the threshold.
As presented in Fig. 8, we observe a consistent behavioral
trend: performance improves as the threshold increases up to
a certain point, after which it begins to degrade. Based on
these results, we identify τsil = 0.02 as the optimal operating
point. The results reveal a clear trade-off governed by the
system’s sensitivity to silence:
Under-detection of Silence (τ sil < 0.02): When the
threshold is set too low, the criterion for identifying silence
becomes excessively strict. This means the system requires
an extremely low confidence score to consider an input as
silence. Consequently, actual silence may fail to be captured
(false negative).
Over-detection of Silence ( τsil > 0.04): Conversely,
as the threshold increases, the criterion becomes overly
permissive. Consequently, predictions that are not actually
silence may be incorrectly selected as silence (false positive).
This leads to signal loss, where potentially informative spatial
cues are discarded, resulting in performance degradation.
Therefore, the choice of τsil = 0.02 serves as an effective
equilibrium, balancing the risks of false negative (too
strict) versus false positive (too permissive). This uniform
stability across diverse faults demonstrates that our proposed
TTA framework is robust without requiring fault-specific
hyperparameter tuning.

performance degradation before TTA, stable adaptation
performance is maintained within a slightly narrower but still
broad range of 0.1 to 0.7. A mild degradation is observed only
at high threshold values (τ t ≥ 0.8), where the overly strict
filtering criterion rejects too many valid predictions, leading
to insufficient supervision signals for adaptation.
In contrast, Signal Replication exhibits greater sensitivity
to the threshold choice, yielding stable performance only
within the range of approximately 0.1 to 0.5. This is
consistent with our earlier observation that replicated signals
generate deceptive yet low-confidence spatial cues, shifting
the model’s overall confidence distribution toward lower
values. Consequently, a high static threshold tends to
excessively filter out these already-degraded predictions,
resulting in a marked performance decline for τt ≥ 0.6.
These findings provide a clear empirical basis for validat-
ing our threshold initialization and dynamic update strategies
described in Sec. IV -B3. As reported in Sec. VI-B3, the
adaptive initialization with Nbuffer = 8 consistently estimates
τ0 within the ranges of 0.4–0.5 for Signal Replication and
0.75–0.80 for Continuous Noise, both of which fall squarely
within the stable operating regions identified in this analysis.

8) ANALYSIS OF TTA DEGRADATION UNDER MICROPHONE
FAULT DETECTION FAILURE
To verify the robustness of the proposed framework under
misdiagnosis, we analyze the system’s behavior when the
fault diagnosis module produces a false positive. Specifically,
we simulate a scenario where the true fault is Impulsive
Noise—the fault type that induced the least performance
degradation among all evaluated conditions (Table 3)—but
is misdetected as Signal Replication. This represents the
worst-case false positive scenario, as it triggers an unneces-
sary channel masking intervention, silencing a channel that,
despite containing impulsive noise, still retains substantial
spatial information, thereby maximizing the potential loss
of valid spatial cues. We assume that after the initial
misdetection persists for K = 2 consecutive frames and
triggers the erroneous intervention, the correct fault type is
re-diagnosed after a varying duration of 10s, 60s, 300s, and
600s, at which point the intervention is released.
Table 5 summarizes the results. Remarkably, even under
the most prolonged misdetection scenario of 600 seconds
(10 minutes), the performance degradation remains marginal,
with the MAE increasing by only 0.15◦ (from 2.99◦ to 3.14◦)
and the accuracy declining by merely 0.34% (from 92.85%
to 92.51%). For shorter misdetection durations, the impact
is negligible; at 10 seconds, the performance is virtually
identical to the correctly diagnosed baseline.
These results highlight two important properties of the
proposed framework. First, such a prolonged continuous
misdetection is highly unlikely in practice, given the
near-perfect diagnosis accuracy demonstrated in Table 2.
Second, even in this extreme hypothetical scenario, the
self-training process driven by confidence-aware pseudo-
labeling partially compensates for the erroneous intervention,
limiting the performance degradation to a negligible level.
It should be noted, however, that this analysis is conducted
under the implicit assumption that the fault diagnosis module
eventually re-diagnoses the correct fault type within a finite
duration—an assumption that holds for the predefined fault
types evaluated in this work, where the fault signatures are
sufficiently distinct to enable eventual correct classification.
For out-of-distribution fault types whose signatures do not
resemble any predefined category, this self-correction mech-
anism may not activate, and prolonged misdetection beyond
the evaluated range could occur. That said, we acknowledge
that the current diagnosis module is trained on a predefined
set of fault types, and its behavior under unexpected
or out-of-distribution hardware failures remains an open
challenge. We identify the development of more probabilistic
and reversible intervention mechanisms for such unseen
fault conditions as an important direction for future work
(Sec. VII).

VII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORKS
In this paper, we proposed a comprehensive Fault-Resilient
SSL framework that ensures reliable robotic perception even
under severe hardware failures. By tightly integrating a
Lightweight Frequency-Time Projection Network for fault
diagnosis with a Diagnosis-Guided Confidence-Aware TTA
strategy, our system autonomously identifies sensor malfunc-
tions and adapts the model at test-time. Experimental results
demonstrate that the proposed method effectively mitigates
performance degradation, reducing the MAE from 21.99 ◦ to
9.91◦ averaged across diverse models and fault conditions.
Furthermore, we validate the generalizability of the proposed
framework through a real-environment experiment using a
physical microphone array with an authentic hardware fault,
confirming that the framework is not limited to synthetic fault
conditions but extends to real-world hardware failures.
Our findings highlight two critical insights for robust
auditory perception. First, we demonstrated that accurate
fault diagnosis is a prerequisite for effective adaptation;
specifically, our diagnosis-guided intervention strategy—
which masks corrupted channels based on precise fault
classification—was essential for recovering from structural
failures like signal replication, where unsupervised adapta-
tion alone proved insufficient. Second, our confidence-aware
hard pseudo-labeling approach significantly outperformed
entropy minimization and soft-labeling baselines, demon-
strating that selectively retaining reliable predictions while
filtering out ambiguous ones is key to preventing error
accumulation during self-training.
For future work, we plan to extend the scalability and
applicability of this framework in three key directions. First,
as demonstrated in Sec. VI-C, simultaneous failures of mul-
tiple microphones can violate the geometric constraint (M ≥
3) required for reliable 2D DOA estimation, imposing a fun-
damental limitation that cannot be overcome by adaptation
alone. Addressing this challenge likely requires extending
beyond single-modality auditory perception toward multi-
modal approaches—for instance, leveraging complementary
visual or inertial cues to compensate for the spatial informa-
tion lost due to microphone failures, thereby enabling robust
localization even under severe array degradation. Second,
we intend to apply our fault-resilient paradigm to other
array-based tasks, such as multi-channel automatic speech
recognition, to investigate how diagnosis-guided adaptation
can maintain recognition performance in the presence of
hardware degradation.
Third, while the proposed fault diagnosis module achieves
near-perfect accuracy on the predefined set of fault types
evaluated in this work, its reliability may degrade when
confronted with unexpected or out-of-distribution hardware
failures that were not encountered during training. This is
an inherent limitation of classification-based fault diagnosis
approaches, including both the conventional V AD-based
method [10] and our proposed model, as classification-based
formulations are fundamentally constrained to recognizing
fault types seen during training and cannot naturally gener-
alize to unseen fault patterns. In such cases, an erroneous
diagnosis could trigger an inappropriate intervention, poten-
tially compromising localization performance. Although our
analysis in Sec. VI-B8 demonstrates that even prolonged
misdetection results in only marginal performance degrada-
tion, addressing this limitation remains an important open
challenge that goes beyond the scope of classification-based
diagnosis alone. Future work should therefore explore more
probabilistic [47], gradual [48], or reversible interven-
tion [34] mechanisms that can gracefully handle unseen fault
conditions without placing absolute trust in the diagnosis
module’s output.
